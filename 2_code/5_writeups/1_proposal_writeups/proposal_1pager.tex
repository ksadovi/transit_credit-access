  {\large\bfseries\color{uchired} All Aboard? Causal Evidence on the Labor-Market Effects of New Rail Stations}\\[3pt]
  {\normalsize Kyra Sadovi \enspace\textbullet\enspace Harris School of Public Policy, University of Chicago \enspace\textbullet\enspace \href{mailto:ksadovi@uchicago.edu}{ksadovi@uchicago.edu}}
\end{center}
\vspace{2pt}

\h{Motivation.} Do workers change their behavior when given physical access to a new transit station? This paper provides causal evidence on the labor-market effects of new U.S. light rail stations, focusing on the residential node of the commute. While much of the transit literature examines effects at the place of work, I argue there are substantial welfare gains to be measured at the place of residence: transit access expands workers' plausible job-search radii and social networks, increasing referral effects and employment outcomes in receiving neighborhoods. This paper isolates that mechanism and sets up a first stage for downstream residential welfare analysis.

\h{Setting and Data.} I have collected planning and construction records for 230 new light rail stations opened in U.S. cities over the last 20 years, 94 of which are currently open. For each project, I record the initial expected opening date from the Draft Environmental Impact Statement (DEIS)---fixed in federal planning documents years before construction begins---and the actual opening date, yielding a measure of construction delay. I define station catchment areas using isochrones at 5, 15, and 30 minutes of pedestrian travel time and merge these to census-tract-level worker flow data from the public LEHD Origin-Destination Employment Statistics (LODES).

\h{Identification.} The central challenge is that station locations are chosen endogenously. My strategy exploits construction delays as quasi-random variation in opening timing. I construct two time indexes: project time $j$ (calendar year minus initial expected opening year, fixed at the DEIS stage) and event time $k$ (calendar year minus actual opening year). Conditional on project-time and tract fixed effects, I argue that actual opening timing is as-good-as-random---the proximate causes of delay are engineering complications, contracting failures, and subsurface geology, not the economic trajectory of adjacent neighborhoods. The baseline specification is a two-way fixed effects regression of log worker flows on a station-open indicator; for dynamic effects I estimate a full event-study specification with coefficients indexed by $k$ and isochrone level $l$, normalizing to $k = -1$. Covariate balance checks support the identifying assumption: delay length is uncorrelated with pre-treatment tract characteristics or baseline income, and delay variation is distributed across states and systems rather than concentrated in any single agency.

\h{Preliminary Results.} Event-study estimates are encouraging. Pre-opening coefficients on log inflows are flat and indistinguishable from zero, consistent with parallel trends. Following opening, inflows rise steadily and persistently, reaching approximately one log point above baseline by ten years post-opening. The gradual buildup---rather than a discrete jump at opening---is consistent with transit access reshaping residential sorting over time as workers update location decisions in response to improved commute options.

\h{Next Steps.} There are three main issues I'm currently dealing with. First, within-system delay clustering reduces effective variation when all stations in a system share the same delay shock; I am working to accommodate treatment intensity across overlapping isochrones, which also helps address SUTVA concerns in densely networked systems. Second, the current setup is agnostic about whether effects are driven by incumbents or new arrivals; I plan to address this with individual-level residential data. Third, I am extending the analysis toward a residential welfare model, where increased worker flows serve as the instrument for identifying downstream social spillovers---labor market referral effects and related outcomes---treating each station as a residential locus rather than only a workplace locus.
